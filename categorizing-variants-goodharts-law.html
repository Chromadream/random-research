Categorizing Variants of Goodhart's Law

David Manheim & Scott Garrabrant • 2018 • arXiv:1803.04585
AI Alignment Machine Learning Public Policy Causal Inference

1. What Is This Paper About?

Goodhart's Law, in its original formulation, states:

💬
"Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes."
— Charles Goodhart, 1975

In plain English: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The moment you start optimizing for a metric, the very relationship that made the metric useful breaks down.

This paper by Manheim & Garrabrant does something crucial that had not been formally done before: it decomposes Goodhart's Law into a rigorous taxonomy of four distinct failure modes, each with its own causal mechanism, mathematical model, and real-world examples. The authors argue that lumping all metric-overoptimization failures under one umbrella obscures important differences — and that understanding which Goodhart effect is at play is essential for designing robust systems in economics, public policy, machine learning, and AI alignment.

2. The Formal Framework

Before diving into the categories, the authors set up a simple but powerful formalism:

  • System S with possible states s ∈ S.
  • Regulator: a single actor who selects a permissible region A ⊆ S.
  • Goal G(s): the regulator's true objective — a mapping from states to a real number.
  • Proxy (Metric) M(s): the measurable stand-in the regulator actually uses, since G is unobservable or too costly to measure directly.
  • Selection pressure: the regulator chooses a threshold c such that states are only permitted when M(s) ≥ c.

A Goodhart effect is defined as: optimization causes a collapse of the statistical relationship between the intended goal G and the proxy M.

3. The Four Categories — Overview

# Category Core Idea Key Question
1 Regressional Selecting for the proxy also selects for noise; the tails come apart. Is the proxy inherently noisy?
2 Extremal Optimization pushes into regions where the old relationships break. Does the relationship hold in extreme regions?
3 Causal The regulator's own intervention breaks the causal link between proxy and goal. Does acting on the proxy destroy the link?
4 Adversarial Other agents with misaligned goals corrupt the metric. Are there strategic actors gaming the metric?

4. Category 1 — Regressional Goodhart

🔹 "Tails Come Apart"

Definition: When you select for a noisy proxy, you unavoidably select for both the true goal and the noise. In the extreme tail of the distribution, the noise component dominates.

M = G + ε,    where ε ~ Normal(μ, σ²)

A point with a very large M likely has a large G and a large ε. At the extreme, G will be predictably smaller than M — the proxy overstates the goal.

⚠️
Key insight: Regressional Goodhart is fundamental and unavoidable. No matter what measure you choose, if it is an inexact proxy, the proxy and goal will diverge in the tail. You can mitigate it (better proxies, less noise), but you can never eliminate it.

5. Category 2 — Extremal Goodhart

🔹 "Out of Distribution"

Definition: When optimization pushes the system into regions of state-space that are very different from where the relationship between M and G was originally observed or learned.

5.1 Subtype: Model Insufficiency

The learned relationship between M and G is a simplified approximation that is accurate only in the observed region. When selection pressure pushes beyond that region, the approximation breaks.

M = G(sᵢ) + G′(sᵢ) ← simplified model misses higher-order terms

Machine Learning Example

You fit a low-degree polynomial to data. In the observed region, higher-order terms are small. But when you optimize to maximize M, you move into regions where those higher-order terms dominate, and the model's predictions become wildly wrong. This is essentially underfitting + distribution shift.

Public Policy Example

A government simplifies a complex social relationship into a metric (e.g., standardized test scores as a proxy for educational quality). As resources are poured into maximizing that metric, the simplification itself becomes the problem — teachers "teach to the test," and the original relationship between test scores and genuine learning collapses.

5.2 Subtype: Change in Regime

Even if the relationship is perfectly learned for the observed region, the generating process fundamentally changes beyond a certain boundary.

G = { M + x, when M ≤ a { M + y, when M > a ← regime change at threshold a

Physical Example

Wind-speed instruments are calibrated for speeds below their design tolerance. Above that threshold, measurements are systematically biased downward. If you optimize for "measured wind speed" to assess wind-damage risk, you get systematically wrong answers for extreme storms.

Another Example

The relationship between wind speed and height changes above the atmospheric boundary layer. A model learned from lower-altitude data fails entirely above that boundary — a genuine phase transition in the underlying physics.

6. Category 3 — Causal Goodhart

🔹 "The Regulator Breaks It"

Definition: When the regulator intervenes in the system to maximize the metric, the causal pathway connecting proxy to goal is severed or distorted. Unlike Regressional and Extremal Goodhart (which only require selection pressure), Causal Goodhart requires the regulator's own action to change the causal structure.

💡
Critical distinction: Causal Goodhart does not require uncertainty or incorrect models. Even with perfect knowledge of all relationships, the very act of intervention can destroy the proxy-goal link. This is fundamentally a causal problem, not an epistemic one.

6.1 Three Causal Goodhart Mechanisms

🔸 Shared Cause Intervention

The regulator intervenes on a variable X that is a common cause of both M and G.

┌───────┐ │ X │ ← The regulator sets X to a maximal value └──┬──┬─┘ │ │ ▼ ▼ ┌────────┐ ┌────────┐ │ Metric │ │ Goal │ └────────┘ └────────┘ After intervention: Metric ⟂ Goal | X The correlation between M and G collapses.

Example: A teacher trains general test-taking skills (shared cause). Scores on both Math and Reading tests improve, but the correlation between them drops. Further optimization based on one test becomes less informative about the other.

🔸 Intermediary Intervention

The regulator intervenes on a variable X that lies on the causal path from G to M.

┌────────┐ ┌───────┐ ┌────────┐ │ Goal │ ──→ │ X │ ──→ │ Metric │ └────────┘ └───────┘ └────────┘ ↑ Regulator sets X directly After intervention: Goal and Metric become independent. Increasing M no longer requires increasing G.

Example: A company wants to increase revenue (Goal) and uses website visits (Metric). Revenue → Marketing spend → Website visits. If the company directly buys bot traffic to inflate visits, the causal chain is broken — visits increase but revenue doesn't.

🔸 Metric Manipulation

The regulator directly sets the metric M to a desired value, bypassing the goal entirely.

┌────────┐ ┌────────┐ │ Goal │ ? │ Metric │ ← Regulator sets M directly └────────┘ └────────┘ ↑ (causal link severed) The relationship between M and G no longer holds.

Example: A teacher simply changes students' test scores in the gradebook. This does nothing for actual learning (Goal), but makes the metric look perfect — and thus useless.

6.2 Non-Causal Goodhart Effects in Causal Systems

The authors also identify three cases where the regulator misunderstands the causal structure but doesn't intervene. These worsen Regressional or Extremal Goodhart, rather than creating true Causal Goodhart:

CaseWhat the regulator gets wrongResult
Ignored Shared Cause Assumes a direct M→G link, but both share a common cause X Selection on M conditions on X, creating an extremal-like collapse
Ignored Intermediary Assumes M→G is direct, but an unmodeled intermediary adds noise Amplifies regressional Goodhart due to extra error term
Ignored Additional Cause Metric/goal is caused by extra factors not in the model Can worsen regressional or extremal effects depending on the distribution

7. Category 4 — Adversarial Goodhart

🔹 "Other Agents Game the Metric"

Definition: When there are multiple actors with different goals, agents can deliberately exploit or corrupt the metric, triggering any of the above Goodhart effects. This is the most complex category because it involves strategic behavior.

7.1 Adversarial Misalignment (Campbell's Law)

🔸 Campbell's Law (formalized)

Campbell's original formulation: "The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor."

The authors formalize this: the agent chooses their own metric MA after seeing the regulator's choice of MR, and then selects on MA to hijack the regulator's optimization.

M_R = G_R + X (Regulator's proxy = Goal + error) M_A = G_A · X (Agent's metric = their Goal × the error term) The agent selects for high M_A values. The regulator's later selection on M_R then inadvertently creates a correlation between X and the regulator's goal.

Key twist: MA and GA may be uncorrelated over the full state-space, but become positively correlated on the subspace selected by the regulator. The agent exploits the regulator's selection pressure for their own ends.

7.2 The Cobra Effect

🔸 Normal Cobra Effect

Historical anecdote: In colonial India, British authorities offered a bounty for dead cobras to reduce the cobra population. Instead, enterprising locals bred cobras to kill for the reward. When the program was cancelled, breeders released their now-worthless cobras — resulting in more cobras than before.

┌────────┐ ┌────────┐ │ Goal │ │ Metric │ └───┬────┘ └───┬────┘ │ │ │ ┌────┐ │ └───→│ Y │←───┘ └────┘ (Unobserved cause the agent exploits) Regulator sets: G_AR = G_A0 + M_R Agent finds Y and manipulates it to maximize M_R without advancing the original Goal.

In the formal model: the regulator modifies the agent's goal to align with their metric (via incentives). The agent then finds an unobserved causal variable Y and manipulates it to maximize the metric through a causal Goodhart mechanism (metric manipulation, shared cause, or intermediary intervention).

🔸 Non-Causal Cobra Effect

The regulator aligns the agent's goal with the metric, and the agent applies selection pressure (rather than causal intervention). This then triggers extremal or regressional Goodhart effects on the regulator's metric.

G_AR = G_A0 + M_R When the agent applies selection pressure to maximize G_AR, it creates a Goodhart effect on M_R.

Difference from Normal Cobra: Here the agent doesn't find a clever causal loophole — they simply optimize hard enough that the statistical relationship between MR and GR breaks down (regressional or extremal effects).

10. Key Insights and Takeaways

🔑 Insight #1: Goodhart Is Not One Thing

The paper's central contribution is showing that "Goodhart's Law" is actually an umbrella term covering at least four mechanistically distinct failure modes. Conflating them leads to confused diagnosis and ineffective solutions.

🔑 Insight #2: Regressional Goodhart Is Unavoidable

Because all proxies are imperfect, the "tails coming apart" phenomenon is a mathematical inevitability. You can reduce the noise (better proxies), but you can never eliminate it. This has profound implications for AI alignment: even a near-perfect reward model will diverge from true human values under sufficient optimization pressure.

🔑 Insight #3: Causal Goodhart Doesn't Need Uncertainty

Unlike regressional and extremal Goodhart, causal Goodhart can occur even with perfect knowledge. The very act of optimizing changes the system. This is closely related to the Lucas Critique in macroeconomics: policy interventions change the behavior that the policy was based on.

🔑 Insight #4: Adversarial Goodhart Is the Hardest

When other intelligent agents are involved, they can actively search for and exploit any of the above failure modes. This makes adversarial Goodhart the most dangerous category for AI alignment — a sufficiently capable AI will find ways to game any metric it's optimized against.

🔑 Insight #5: Optimization Power Amplifies Everything

The severity of all Goodhart effects scales with the amount of optimization power applied. As AI systems become more capable optimizers, the risk of catastrophic Goodhart failures grows. This makes the taxonomy especially critical for AI alignment research.

12. Conclusion

Manheim & Garrabrant's paper provides a much-needed formal taxonomy for a phenomenon that had previously been discussed in vague and overlapping terms. By decomposing Goodhart's Law into four distinct mechanisms — Regressional, Extremal, Causal, and Adversarial — with clearly specified subcategories, the authors give researchers and practitioners a shared vocabulary and diagnostic framework.

The taxonomy is not merely academic: it has direct practical implications for how we design metrics, incentives, and optimization systems. Understanding which Goodhart effect is at play suggests which mitigations are appropriate:

  • Regressional: Reduce noise in proxies, but accept that some divergence is inevitable.
  • Extremal: Use better models, gather data from extreme regions, add uncertainty quantification.
  • Causal: Model causal structure explicitly; consider the effects of your own interventions.
  • Adversarial: Design robust metrics, anticipate gaming, use multiple diverse metrics, and limit optimization power.

As AI systems become more powerful optimizers, the importance of this taxonomy only grows. The paper is a foundational reference for anyone working on AI alignment, mechanism design, or any domain where metrics are used to guide optimization.
