What Is It Like to Be a Bat?

A Comprehensive Explanation of Thomas Nagel's Landmark 1974 Essay
Published in The Philosophical Review, Vol. 83, No. 4 (Oct. 1974), pp. 435–450

1 Introduction: The Problem That Won't Go Away

Thomas Nagel's "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" is one of the most widely cited and influential essays in the philosophy of mind. Published in 1974, it has shaped virtually all subsequent debates about consciousness, the mind–body problem, and the limits of scientific reductionism.

Nagel's central target is physicalism reductionism — the view that all mental phenomena can be fully explained in terms of physical processes in the brain. He argues that there is something about conscious experience that stubbornly resists this kind of explanation: its subjective character.

Nagel's Opening Gambit

"Consciousness is what makes the mind-body problem really intractable. Perhaps that is why current discussions of the problem give it little attention or get it obviously wrong."
— Thomas Nagel, opening lines

Nagel observes that reductionists love to point to successful scientific reductions — water reduced to H₂O, genes to DNA, lightning to electrical discharge. But he contends that none of these analogies actually illuminate the mind–brain relationship, because consciousness presents a unique kind of explanatory challenge.

2 The Core Thesis: "Something It Is Like"

Nagel introduces what has become the most famous definition of consciousness in modern philosophy:

"An organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is like to be that organism — something it is like for the organism."

This is deceptively simple. What Nagel is pointing to is the subjective character of experience — the first-person, "what it feels like from the inside" quality of conscious life. This is also known in the philosophical literature as qualia, phenomenal consciousness, or raw feels.

Consciousness isn't just about what an organism does — it's about what it feels like to be that organism.

Nagel argues that this subjective character is not captured by any of the standard reductionist analyses of mind:

  • Functional analyses — describing mental states by their causal roles — could apply equally to a robot that behaves like a person but experiences nothing at all.
  • Behavioral analyses — describing mental states in terms of dispositions to behave — miss the fact that experience is present even in animals that lack language and cannot report on their experiences.
  • Intentional analyses — describing mental states in terms of what they are "about" — fail to capture the raw qualitative feel of experience.

3 The Bat: Why This Particular Animal?

Nagel chooses bats deliberately, and with care. He rejects wasps or flounders because "if one travels too far down the phylogenetic tree, people gradually shed their faith that there is experience there at all." Bats are mammals like us — close enough that we believe they have conscious experience — yet their sensory world is radically, almost unimaginably, different from our own.

Echolocation: A Fundamentally Alien Sense

Most bats (the microchiroptera) perceive the world primarily through echolocation: they emit rapid, subtly modulated, high-frequency shrieks and detect the reflections bouncing back from objects. Their brains correlate outgoing impulses with returning echoes, enabling them to make precise discriminations of distance, size, shape, motion, and texture — comparable to what we achieve with vision.

The Imagination Test — And Why It Fails

Nagel asks us to try to imagine what it's like to be a bat. You might try to picture:

  • Having webbing on your arms
  • Flying around at dusk catching insects in your mouth
  • Having poor vision and perceiving the world through reflected sound signals
  • Hanging upside down by your feet in an attic

But here's the crucial insight: all of this only tells you what it would be like for you to behave as a bat behaves. It doesn't tell you what it's like for the bat to be a bat.

The Distinction That Changes Everything

What I CAN Imagine What I CANNOT Imagine
What it would be like for me to behave as a bat behaves What it is like for the bat to be a bat
I project my own human sensory apparatus onto bat-like activities I would need to inhabit the bat's actual sensory phenomenology — echolocation as lived experience
Additions and subtractions to my experience A fundamentally different mode of consciousness, not reachable by modifying mine

Even if you could gradually metamorphose into a bat, "nothing in my present constitution enables me to imagine what the experiences of such a future stage of myself thus metamorphosed would be like." The resources of your own mind are simply inadequate to the task.

4 The Problem of Other Minds — And Beyond

Crucially, Nagel is not a skeptic about bat consciousness. He firmly believes bats do have experiences. His point is epistemological and conceptual: we can know that bats have experiences without being able to know what those experiences are like.

He extends this reasoning to inter-species understanding generally. Imagine intelligent bats or Martians trying to understand human consciousness:

Nagel draws a striking conclusion from this: there may be facts beyond the reach of human concepts. Just as there would have been transfinite numbers even if Cantor had died before discovering them, there may be facts about bat phenomenology — or Martian phenomenology — that humans can never represent or comprehend, simply because our cognitive structure doesn't permit it.

"To deny the reality or logical significance of what we can never describe or understand is the crudest form of cognitive dissonance."

5 The Clash of Perspectives: Subjective vs. Objective

This is the philosophical engine of Nagel's paper. He identifies a structural feature of conscious experience that makes it uniquely resistant to reduction:

The Crucial Distinction

Subjective facts about experience are essentially tied to a particular point of view. You can only fully grasp what it's like to see red, or feel pain, or echolocate a moth, by occupying (or being able to imaginatively adopt) the point of view of the experiencer.

Objective facts, by contrast, are accessible from many different points of view. The neurophysiology of a bat's brain, the physics of echolocation, the chemistry of lightning — these can be understood by humans, Martians, or intelligent bats alike, regardless of their particular sensory apparatus.

Why Lightning Is Different From Experience

Nagel uses lightning as a powerful contrast case:

Lightning (Reducible) Conscious Experience (Not Obviously Reducible)
A Martian without vision can understand lightning as an electrical discharge A Martian cannot understand what it's like to see lightning by studying my brain
The visual appearance of lightning is not part of what lightning is; it's an effect on observers The subjective feel of experience is the experience; there is no appearance/reality distinction to exploit
Moving to a more objective viewpoint gets you closer to the real nature of lightning Moving to a more objective viewpoint takes you farther away from the real nature of experience

This is the devastating point: in every successful scientific reduction, we leave behind the species-specific subjective appearance and describe what the thing is "in itself," objectively. But with consciousness, the subjective appearance IS the reality. If you leave it behind, you've left behind the very thing you were trying to explain.

The Point-of-View Test

Nagel poses a devastating question: "What would be left of what it was like to be a bat if one removed the viewpoint of the bat?" The answer seems to be: nothing. The subjective character of experience is inseparable from the point of view of the experiencing subject. But physical science deals precisely in what remains after you've abstracted away from any particular point of view.

6 What Nagel Is — and Isn't — Arguing Against

It's essential to understand what Nagel is not saying:

What Nagel Does NOT Claim

  • ❌ He does not claim that physicalism is false.
  • ❌ He does not claim that bats lack consciousness.
  • ❌ He does not claim that we can never understand consciousness.
  • ❌ He does not endorse dualism (mind as a separate substance).

Rather, his argument is that physicalism — the thesis that mental states are physical states of the brain — is a position we cannot currently understand, because we lack the conceptual framework that would make it intelligible.

"It would be a mistake to conclude that physicalism must be false. Nothing is proved by the inadequacy of physicalist hypotheses that assume a faulty objective analysis of mind. It would be truer to say that physicalism is a position we cannot understand because we do not at present have any conception of how it might be true."

The "Matter Is Energy" Analogy

Nagel draws a brilliant parallel: if a pre-Socratic philosopher had declared "all matter is really energy," they would have been saying something that turned out to be true — but they would have had no conception of what made it true. They lacked the theoretical framework (relativity, E=mc², quantum mechanics) that makes the identity intelligible. Nagel suggests we are in exactly that position with "mental states are brain states."

The word "is" is deceptively simple, Nagel warns. It doesn't, by itself, give you any idea of how two things could be the same thing. That requires a conceptual background — and in the case of mind and brain, we don't have it.

7 Nagel's Speculative Proposal: "Objective Phenomenology"

Nagel closes with a constructive suggestion. Even if we can't reduce the subjective to the physical using current concepts, perhaps we can develop new concepts that bridge the gap from the other direction:

"At present we are completely unequipped to think about the subjective character of experience without relying on the imagination — without taking up the point of view of the experiential subject. This should be regarded as a challenge to form new concepts and devise a new method — an objective phenomenology not dependent on empathy or the imagination."

The goal of such a phenomenology would be to describe the subjective character of experiences in a form comprehensible even to beings incapable of having those experiences. A blind-from-birth person might come to understand something structural about vision; a human might come to understand something structural about echolocation. The loose intermodal analogies we currently rely on ("red is like the sound of a trumpet") are clearly inadequate — but perhaps structural features of perception could be described objectively, even if something is inevitably left out.

8 Criticisms and Responses

Nagel's paper has generated enormous debate. Here are the major lines of criticism:

1. Daniel Dennett: Consciousness Explained (or Explained Away)

Dennett, perhaps Nagel's most prominent critic, argues that the "what it is like" quality of consciousness is a kind of user illusion. He contends that any "interesting or theoretically important" features of a bat's consciousness would be accessible to third-person scientific investigation. For Dennett, there is no irreducible residue; the sense that there is one is a cognitive illusion produced by the way our brains represent things to themselves.

2. Kathleen Akins: The Neuroscience We Don't Yet Know

Akins argues that Nagel is too quick to declare bat consciousness inaccessible. Many open questions about bat subjective experience actually hinge on unanswered neuroscientific questions — for example, the function of various cortical activity profiles in the active bat brain. Once we understand the neuroscience better, we may find that the gap between objective knowledge and subjective understanding shrinks considerably.

3. P.M.S. Hacker: A Linguistic Confusion

Hacker argues that Nagel's entire framing is "malconstructed" and philosophically "misconceived." He contends that "consciousness" is not a thing that can be defined by a "what it is like" locution, and that Nagel's paper "laid the groundwork for… forty years of fresh confusion about consciousness."

4. Schwitzgebel & Gordon: Humans DO Echolocate

Schwitzgebel and Gordon have argued that normal sighted humans actually do use echolocation — often without being aware of it. They point to phenomena like the "facial vision" of blind individuals and suggest that normal people can be grossly mistaken about their own conscious experience. If humans can echolocate, the bat's sensory world may not be as alien as Nagel supposed.

9 Legacy and Influence

Nagel's essay has become one of the most cited and discussed papers in all of philosophy. Its influence extends far beyond academic philosophy into neuroscience, psychology, and consciousness studies:

  • David Chalmers' "Hard Problem" — The distinction between the "easy problems" of consciousness (explaining behavior, information processing, etc.) and the "hard problem" (explaining subjective experience) is a direct descendant of Nagel's argument.
  • Frank Jackson's "Mary's Room" — The thought experiment about a color scientist who knows all the physical facts about color but has never seen red is a close cousin of Nagel's bat argument, making essentially the same point about the limits of physical knowledge.
  • Joseph Levine's "Explanatory Gap" — Levine's argument that there is an unbridgeable gap between physical explanations and phenomenal experience directly echoes Nagel.
  • The "What It's Like" Locution — Nagel's phrase has become the standard way of defining consciousness in the philosophical and scientific literature. It is almost impossible to find a paper on consciousness that doesn't cite or echo it.

The Paper's Enduring Question

Nagel doesn't give us an answer. He gives us a question — and a framework for understanding why the question is so hard. The bat is a mirror: in trying to imagine its inner life, we discover the limits of our own concepts, our own imagination, and our own scientific methods. And that discovery, Nagel thinks, is the first step toward genuinely understanding consciousness.

Summary: The Argument in Seven Steps

  1. Consciousness has a subjective character. There is something it is like to be a conscious organism — something it is like for that organism.
  2. This subjective character is tied to a point of view. You can only fully grasp the subjective character of an experience by adopting (or being able to adopt) the point of view of the experiencer.
  3. Physical science abstracts away from all particular points of view. It describes the world as it is from no perspective in particular — the "view from nowhere."
  4. Therefore, physical descriptions seem to leave out the subjective character of experience. If you describe only what can be observed from any point of view, you describe everything except what it's like from a particular point of view.
  5. This is unlike any other scientific reduction. When we reduce lightning to electrical discharge, the visual appearance is an effect on observers, not part of what lightning is. But with experience, the subjective appearance is the reality.
  6. Physicalism may still be true — but we currently have no conception of how it could be true. We lack the conceptual framework to make the identity "mental state = brain state" intelligible.
  7. We need new concepts. Nagel proposes the development of an "objective phenomenology" — a way of describing the structural features of experience that doesn't depend on empathy or imagination, and that might eventually make a physical theory of mind intelligible.
