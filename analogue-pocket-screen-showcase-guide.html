If you only try ten

One showpiece per trick the screen can do: the 10× grid, the LCD recreations, raw versus "as-designed" colour, and the adapter systems.

    How much of the screen each system fills

    Largest whole-number scale that fits 1600×1440. Integer scaling is why the pixel-grid modes look so convincing. Share of the panel used:

    PC Engine figure assumes the common 256×224 mode; the system can change resolution per game, so some HuCards scale differently. Atari Lynx at 10× uses the full width; vertical Lynx games rotate instead. WonderSwan portrait games are turned upright by the core, so the panel's height caps them at 6× — about half the screen.

    Getting the most out of the panel

    Flip modes mid-game

    Hold Analogue and press ← / →. The fastest way to compare raw colour with the original LCD on the same scene.

    Frame blending for flicker

    Old LCDs smeared frames, so games flickered sprites to fake transparency. Turn blending on for those; leave it off elsewhere, as it softens motion.

    Raw vs "Original" colour

    GBC and early GBA games pushed saturation to survive unlit screens. "Analogue" modes show the raw data; "Original" modes show what the artists actually saw.

    Rotation & tate

    The display rotates a full 360°, which is what you want for vertical Lynx games. The WonderSwan core rotates portrait games itself.

    Adapter wobble

    HuCards and Lynx carts stick out of their adapters; a knock can crash the game. Play those seated, or from a flash cart.

    Your own palettes

    Analogue GB mode takes custom palettes, so monochrome games can wear any four colours without the grid.
